If people must book a time at a counter, a station, or a field visit, and two of them must never
get the same place, [Registry Scheduling](../../reference/glossary/#registry-scheduling) publishes
what can be booked and keeps every booking in one ledger.

## What a caller gets

A caller reads the [offerings](../../reference/glossary/#offering) a deployment publishes and the
free starts for one. A start whose [pool](../../reference/glossary/#resource-pool) members
are all taken leaves the list and is never shown with `free: 0`.

The caller takes a [hold](../../reference/glossary/#hold) on a start with `POST /v1/holds`. The hold
claims capacity from one pool member at once and expires on its own. Confirming it with
`POST /v1/appointments` turns it into an [appointment](../../reference/glossary/#appointment) on the
same member. A caller that needs no pause can book directly by sending the full request instead of
a hold. Rescheduling and cancelling name the appointment revision they acted on and advance it. The
[API reference](../../reference/apis/registry-scheduling/#contract-boundary) gives the appointment's
state and revision.

## What you define

Three files, each with its own owner and lifecycle:

- `scheduling.yaml`, the booking policy: services, offerings, openings, holiday sets, the hold
  policy, reminders, and observer hooks. `schedulingctl package` turns it into a package with a
  digest, and the runtime refuses to start on any other.
- `records.yaml`, the supply: locations and their time zones,
  [resource pools](../../reference/glossary/#resource-pool) of interchangeable stations or staff and
  their members, arrival windows, and dated closures. The operator applies records to the database
  while the service runs.
- The runtime file: the listener, PostgreSQL, the token issuer, the audit log, and where reminders
  and hook events go.

`schedulingctl` checks the policy and records offline, replays your
[fixtures](../../reference/glossary/#scheduling-fixture) against them, and packages the policy.

Each activated package gets a policy revision. Callers read the current one from
`GET /v1/scheduling` and send it with every booking, so a policy change between reading and booking
is refused with `policy.changed` rather than applied silently.

## What runs

`scheduling` is an HTTP service that keeps the ledger in PostgreSQL 17 or newer and verifies tokens
from your own OpenID Connect issuer. Every hold and appointment is a claim against the capacity
ledger, made inside one PostgreSQL transaction. Two callers racing for the last station get one
appointment and one `capacity.exhausted`, never two appointments.

Around the ledger, the service keeps an outbox. A confirmed appointment records the reminder intents
its offering declares and, when the policy has hooks, the observer events. Workers deliver them
after the transaction commits, so a slow receiver never holds a station.

## What Scheduling refuses

Scheduling refuses a booking without a [task grant](../../reference/glossary/#task-grant) for that
service, location, and action, a start outside the published hours, a booking made against a policy
revision that has changed, and a start with no free capacity.

A task grant is a signed claim in the caller's access token. It names a service, a location, and the
actions allowed there, such as `hold.create` or `appointment.create`. The system that owns the
eligibility rule issues it, for example from a Registry Casework task template a staff member
approves. A token with read scope but no grant can read availability and is refused a booking with
`403 operation.not-authorized`. [Task grants](../../reference/apis/registry-scheduling/#task-grants) sets out how
Scheduling matches and re-checks one.

## Recovering an unknown outcome

A booking call can time out after it committed. Each create, reschedule, and cancel carries an
`Idempotency-Key` the caller chooses. The same request under the same key returns the first answer,
never a second booking. A different request under that key is refused with `idempotency.key-reused`.
The Rust client in `registry-scheduling-client` retries this way.

When the grant has expired, a retry would be refused, so read instead. The receipt route takes the
original key and body under an ordinary read token.
[Recover an unknown outcome](../../reference/apis/registry-scheduling/#recover-an-unknown-outcome)
gives each request, and
[Deploy and operate Registry Scheduling](../../operate/scheduling/) covers `schedulingctl status`
and `schedulingctl intents`.

## Who owns a booking

Scheduling owns the ledger and nothing beside it. No other product writes a hold or an appointment,
directly or through a shared database. Each hold and appointment belongs to the token issuer and
subject that made it. Another caller, even one serving the same product, sees an empty list. The
appointment carries the `externalReferences` the caller sent, naming the record it was booked for.

## When it is not the right product

Scheduling does not decide who may have a service, because the system that owns that rule does. It
is not a work queue, which is [Registry Casework](../casework/). It sends no messages: a reminder or
an observer event leaves as data, and the message, the channel, and the decision to send anything
belong to a receiver such as [Registry Messaging](../messaging/).
